Declaration of co-ownership: what it contains and how to amend it

November 12, 2026  ·  7-min read  ·  By Guillaume Prentki

The declaration of co-ownership is the founding document of your condo building. It sets each owner's share of the common expenses, the number of votes at the general meeting, what is common and what is private, and the building's rules. It binds all co-owners, present and future. Here is what it contains, what it can restrict and how to amend it under the Civil Code of Québec.

Key takeaways
  • The declaration has 3 parts: the act constituting the co-ownership, the by-laws of the immovable and the description of the fractions.
  • It is made before a notary and registered in the land register. It binds the co-owners and their successors from the time of registration.
  • The by-laws are amended by a majority of the votes of the co-owners present or represented at the meeting.
  • The act constituting the co-ownership and the description of the fractions are amended by co-owners representing three-quarters of the votes of those present or represented, then by notarial act.
  • Changing the destination of the building requires three-quarters of the co-owners representing 90% of the votes of all co-owners.

The 3 parts of the declaration of co-ownership

Under the Civil Code of Québec, a declaration of co-ownership comprises three separate documents (article 1052).

1. The act constituting the co-ownership

This is the core of the declaration. It defines (article 1053):

  • the destination of the building, the private portions and the common portions (for example, residential use only);
  • the relative value of each fraction and how it was determined;
  • each fraction's share of the common expenses;
  • the number of votes attached to each fraction;
  • the respective powers and duties of the board of directors and of the general meeting of co-owners;
  • any other agreement regarding the building, including penal clauses for breaches of the declaration.

2. The by-laws of the immovable

These are the building's rules of living (article 1054): the enjoyment, use and maintenance of the private and common portions, the operation and administration of the co-ownership, and the procedure for assessing and collecting contributions to the common expenses. This is where you usually find the rules on noise, pets, renovations, parking or common areas.

The by-laws also apply to the tenant or occupant of a unit, once they are given a copy by the co-owner or, failing that, by the syndicate (article 1057). An owner who rents out their condo should therefore give the by-laws to the tenant.

3. The description of the fractions

It contains the cadastral description of the private and common portions, and a description of the real rights charging the building or existing in its favour, such as servitudes (article 1055).

What the declaration can restrict, and what it cannot

A declaration of co-ownership may not impose any restriction on the co-owners' rights except restrictions justified by the destination, characteristics or location of the building (article 1056). A restriction that goes further can be challenged.

In practice, a restriction on the use of units, pets or rentals must be tied to the destination or characteristics of the building. Borderline cases are often decided by the courts: if in doubt, consult a notary or lawyer before adopting a new rule.

Two important protections for co-owners:

  • any clause in the declaration that changes the number of votes required by law for a decision is deemed unwritten (article 1101);
  • any decision that, contrary to the declaration, imposes on a co-owner a change in the relative value of their fraction or in the destination of their private portion is without effect (article 1102).

How to amend the declaration of co-ownership

The required majority depends on which part of the declaration you want to change.

What you want to amendRequired majorityForm
By-laws of the immovable, or a clerical errorMajority of the votes of the co-owners present or represented (art. 1096)Express amendment in the minutes or a written resolution, filed in the syndicate's register (art. 1060)
Act constituting the co-ownership or description of the fractionsCo-owners representing three-quarters of the votes of those present or represented (art. 1097)Notarial act signed by the syndicate, registered in the land register (art. 1059 and 1060)
Destination of the buildingThree-quarters of the co-owners representing 90% of the votes of all co-owners (art. 1098)Notarial act, registered in the land register

The three-quarters majority also applies to other major decisions: alteration, enlargement or improvement work on the common portions, purchases or sales of immovables by the syndicate, and the construction of buildings to create new fractions (article 1097).

Steps to amend the declaration

  1. Draft the amendment with the board of directors, and with a notary if it affects the act constituting the co-ownership or the description of the fractions.
  2. Call the meeting and attach the proposed text to the notice, so co-owners can vote with full knowledge.
  3. Vote with the required majority and record the decision in the minutes.
  4. Make it official: file it in the syndicate's register for the by-laws; notarial act signed by the syndicate and registration in the land register for the act constituting the co-ownership and the description of the fractions. Amendments are filed exclusively in French.
  5. Inform the co-owners and give the amended by-laws to tenants and occupants.

A co-owner may ask the court to annul a decision of the general meeting that is biased, taken with intent to injure the co-owners or in contempt of their rights, or affected by an error in counting the votes. The action must be brought within 90 days after the meeting (article 1103). That is why a properly called meeting and a careful vote count matter.

Where to find your building's declaration

  • In the syndicate's register: the syndicate must keep the declaration of co-ownership, as well as the by-laws and their amendments (article 1070). Ask your board of directors or your condo manager.
  • In the Québec land register: the declaration and amendments to the act constituting the co-ownership and the description of the fractions are registered there. Amendments to the by-laws are not: they are only in the syndicate's register.
  • From your notary: you normally received a copy when you bought your condo.

Is your declaration decades old and no longer reflects how the building works? That is common. A well-prepared update prevents disputes and makes unit sales easier. We support boards with these steps as part of our administrative and legal condo management.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the declaration of co-ownership and the by-laws?

The by-laws of the immovable are one of the three parts of the declaration of co-ownership, along with the act constituting the co-ownership and the description of the fractions. The by-laws contain the building's rules of living, and they are the easiest part to amend: a majority of the votes of the co-owners present or represented is enough.

Do you need a notary to amend the declaration of co-ownership?

Yes for the act constituting the co-ownership and the description of the fractions: the amendment must be a notarial act, signed by the syndicate and registered in the land register. No for the by-laws: an amendment adopted at the meeting, recorded in the minutes or in a written resolution, is simply filed in the syndicate's register.

Do the by-laws apply to tenants?

Yes, as soon as the tenant or occupant is given a copy of the by-laws, or of the amendments to them, by the co-owner or, failing that, by the syndicate.

Is a new owner bound by the declaration?

Yes. The declaration of co-ownership binds the co-owners and their successors, including buyers, from the time of its registration in the land register.

Can an amendment adopted at a meeting be challenged?

Yes. A co-owner may ask the court to annul a decision that is biased, taken with intent to injure the co-owners or in contempt of their rights, or affected by an error in counting the votes. The action must be brought within 90 days after the meeting.
